Influence that answered back

Dialogues

Most influence flows one way. Sometimes the records talk back — two works feeding each other across a scene or a year. Each direction carries its own receipt.

Reading rooms: receipts · moments

  • Milestones 1958 ⇄ Kind of Blue 1959
    → Davis had already tried modal composition on Milestones before making this album.
    ← The album is grouped with Kind of Blue as part of Davis’s modal phase.
  • Gone With the Wind 1959 ⇄ Time Out 1959
    → Columbia backed Time Out only after the quartet first recorded Gone with the Wind.
    ← The label wanted a conventional album first to offset the risk of the quartet’s planned Time Out project.
  • Blowin’ Your Mind! 1967 ⇄ Astral Weeks 1968
    → Morrison was performing songs from Blowin’ Your Mind! while developing the album’s acoustic approach.
    ← AllMusic says this album was a dry run for Astral Weeks.
  • Are You Experienced 1967 ⇄ Axis: Bold as Love 1967
    → Ferris attended sessions for the next album and used that music as part of his inspiration for the cover concept.
    ← Karl Ferris said hearing this album helped inspire the space-travel concept for the later cover shoot.
  • Are You Experienced 1967 ⇄ Axis: Bold as Love 1967
    → Critics saw Axis as showing more growth and a looser, jazzier, more expansive sound than Are You Experienced.
    ← Karl Ferris said hearing this album helped inspire the space-travel concept for the later cover shoot.
  • Their Satanic Majesties Request 1967 ⇄ Between the Buttons 1967
    → Oldham was the last producer to handle a Stones album before they split with him during work on Their Satanic Majesties Request.
    ← Recording for this album started right after Between the Buttons came out.
  • Dusty in Memphis 1969 ⇄ From Elvis in Memphis 1969
    → The album is described as sharing the same Memphis-era trends as Dusty Springfield's Dusty in Memphis.
    ← It was the first of a wave of Memphis-style albums; Elvis Presley's From Elvis in Memphis followed with recordings at American.
  • Black Sabbath 1970 ⇄ Master of Reality 1971
    → The album is described as more experimental than the band’s first two records.
    ← Iommi said he later downtuned the guitar on Master of Reality, extending the playing approach that shaped this album.
  • Déjà vu 1970 ⇄ After the Gold Rush 1970
    → The album's success helped Neil Young's solo album After the Gold Rush.
    ← The album was one of four high-profile solo releases that followed CSNY’s Déjà Vu.
  • Déjà vu 1970 ⇄ After the Gold Rush 1970
    → The album’s popularity helped the success of Neil Young’s next album, After the Gold Rush.
    ← The album was one of four high-profile solo releases that followed CSNY’s Déjà Vu.
  • American Beauty 1970 ⇄ Workingman’s Dead 1970
    → Workingman’s Dead and American Beauty were recorded back-to-back in a similar style, with both albums sharing the same country-leaning direction.
    ← The album continued the folk rock and country style of Workingman's Dead.
  • Hunky Dory 1971 ⇄ The Man Who Sold the World 1970
    → Its sequencing, atmosphere, and songwriting are said to predate Hunky Dory.
    ← The album's success led Bowie to write much of Hunky Dory.
  • Hunky Dory 1971 ⇄ The Man Who Sold the World 1970
    → Its sequencing, atmosphere, and songwriting are said to predate Hunky Dory.
    ← The album shifts away from the harder guitar sound of The Man Who Sold the World toward warmer piano-led pop and art pop.
  • Veedon Fleece 1974 ⇄ Hard Nose the Highway 1973
    → Erik Hage says the album was criticized partly because Veedon Fleece came soon after it.
    ← "Country Fair" was left over from Hard Nose the Highway and reused here.
  • Tonight’s the Night 1975 ⇄ On the Beach 1974
    → It shares Tonight's the Night's bleakness and rough production, and was made after that album.
    ← Alternate versions of Tonight’s the Night incorporated songs from the On the Beach sessions.
  • Fleetwood Mac 1975 ⇄ Heroes Are Hard to Find 1974
    → The cover photographer later also supplied the artwork for Fleetwood Mac.
    ← Fleetwood Mac recorded the album after relocating to California and finishing their previous album.
  • A New World Record 1976 ⇄ Out of the Blue 1977
    → The spaceship cover was based on the logo Kosh designed for ELO’s previous album, A New World Record.
    ← Bruce Eder compared it with ELO’s later Out of the Blue and judged this album the stronger one.
  • The Idiot 1977 ⇄ Low 1977
    → Bowie produced and co-wrote Iggy Pop's The Idiot, which influenced the sound of Low.
    ← Critics noted that Low’s electronic sound had been pioneered on The Idiot.
  • Duke 1980 ⇄ Face Value 1981
    → Some songs Collins wrote for Face Value were later used by Genesis on Duke.
    ← Collins wrote material during the hiatus, and some of it was later reworked for Face Value.
  • Duke 1980 ⇄ Face Value 1981
    → Some songs Collins wrote for Face Value were later used by Genesis on Duke.
    ← The album’s unused songs “Misunderstanding” and “Please Don’t Ask” were later used on Genesis’s Duke.
  • Face Value 1981 ⇄ Peter Gabriel 1980
    → Nick Launay later worked on Phil Collins’s Face Value after assisting with the sound associated with this album.
    ← The album's sound influenced Phil Collins' debut studio album.
  • Face Value 1981 ⇄ Grace and Danger 1980
    → The album is closely kin to Phil Collins’s Face Value, which also centers on relationship breakdown.
    ← Collins said he was influenced by the divorce-and-breakup narrative on John Martyn’s Grace and Danger while making Face Value.
  • Mirage 1982 ⇄ Bella Donna 1981
    → Nicks said her tears on the 1981 live clip came from knowing she would soon join Fleetwood Mac to record Mirage.
    ← Stan Lynch recalled rehearsing “Gypsy” during these sessions, and it was later recorded by Fleetwood Mac.
  • Double Nickels on the Dime 1984 ⇄ Zen Arcade 1984
    → Minutemen’s double album was made in response to Hüsker Dü’s Zen Arcade.
    ← Zen Arcade inspired Minutemen to make their own double album, Double Nickels on the Dime.
  • All or Nothing 1988 ⇄ Girl You Know It’s True 1989
    → Frank Farian said Clive Davis decided to repackage All or Nothing as Girl You Know It's True for North America.
    ← The album was replaced in the U.S. by Girl You Know It's True, which kept four of the same songs.
  • Dr. Feelgood 1989 ⇄ Pump 1989
    → Aerosmith were in the studio while Mötley Crüe recorded Dr. Feelgood, and Aerosmith members added backing vocals and instrumentation to it.
    ← Steven Tyler was in Vancouver recording Pump while contributing backing vocals to the album.
  • Doggystyle 1993 ⇄ The Chronic 1992
    → A retrospective said The Chronic and Doggystyle made gangsta life sound playful.
    ← Snoop said Doggystyle grew out of the realism and street-life perspective he had already developed on The Chronic.
  • Only Built 4 Cuban Linx… 1995 ⇄ Liquid Swords 1995
    → The article says “I Gotcha’ Back” was similar to Raekwon’s “Heaven & Hell” because both first appeared on the Fresh soundtrack.
    ← Some beats, including one for “Incarcerated Scarfaces,” were first meant for Liquid Swords.
  • Only Built 4 Cuban Linx… 1995 ⇄ Liquid Swords 1995
    → RZA and GZA started writing and recording Liquid Swords while Raekwon and RZA were finishing Only Built 4 Cuban Linx....
    ← Some beats, including one for “Incarcerated Scarfaces,” were first meant for Liquid Swords.
  • Myra Lee 1996 ⇄ Dear Sir 1995
    → The same 1994 New York sessions produced material for both Dear Sir and Myra Lee.
    ← The album was recorded in the same sessions as Dear Sir.
  • Homework 1997 ⇄ Da Funk 1995
    → Homework's last track, 'Funk Ad', is a 50-second reversed sample of 'Da Funk'.
    ← Daft Punk returned to the studio to record Da Funk before Homework was released.
  • The Slim Shady LP 1999 ⇄ The Marshall Mathers LP 2000
    → The album’s “Public Service Announcement” intro was later echoed on The Marshall Mathers LP.
    ← “97 Bonnie & Clyde” is described as an epilogue to “Kim.”
  • The Slim Shady LP 1999 ⇄ The Marshall Mathers LP 2000
    → The album is described as a follow-up that reacts to the earlier record’s success and controversy.
    ← “97 Bonnie & Clyde” is described as an epilogue to “Kim.”
  • The Sweetest Punch 1999 ⇄ Painted From Memory 1998
    → Costello said Bacharach’s unexpected piano idea helped them finish “I Still Have That Other Girl.”
    ← Frisell’s album was issued as a companion to Painted from Memory, and many of that album’s songs were reworked here.
  • The Sweetest Punch 1999 ⇄ Painted From Memory 1998
    → Frisell’s companion album was built from Painted from Memory songs, using demos and independent jazz arrangements.
    ← Frisell’s album was issued as a companion to Painted from Memory, and many of that album’s songs were reworked here.
  • Rated R 2000 ⇄ Cocaine Rodeo 2000
    → "13th Floor" was later re-recorded as "Tension Head" for Queens of the Stone Age's Rated R.
    ← “Tension Head” re-records Mondo Generator’s “13th Floor.”
  • Rated R 2000 ⇄ Cocaine Rodeo 2000
    → "13th Floor" was later re-recorded as "Tension Head" for Queens of the Stone Age's Rated R.
    ← “Tension Head” is a re-recording of “13th Floor” from Mondo Generator’s Cocaine Rodeo.
  • Reality 2003 ⇄ Heathen 2002
    → One track was said to foreshadow Bowie’s next album, Reality.
    ← One track on Heathen was said to foreshadow Reality.
  • Reality 2003 ⇄ Heathen 2002
    → One track was said to foreshadow Bowie’s next album, Reality.
    ← The urban setting of the recording is said to have influenced the new material, much as Allaire Studios influenced Heathen.
  • The Grey Album 2004 ⇄ The Black Album 2003
    → Danger Mouse’s mashup used The Black Album vocals with Beatles instrumentals.
    ← The acappella release also led producers to create The Grey Album.
  • Late Registration 2005 ⇄ The College Dropout 2004
    → A journalist described this album as the first in West's pop-rap trilogy, followed by Late Registration.
    ← The album moves away from the sped-up soul sampling style of West’s debut.
